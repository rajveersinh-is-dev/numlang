\begin{abstract}
Supercompilation, introduced by Valentin Turchin in 1986, is a powerful program transformation technique capable of global algorithmic optimizations, cross-boundary function fusion, and deforestation. However, practical adoption in optimizing systems compilers has been hindered by three historic challenges: (1) state explosion and unpredictable compilation time, (2) the absence of formal mechanical guarantees of semantic soundness and guaranteed termination on imperative control-flow graphs, and (3) difficulties in realizing self-application and automated compiler generation (the 2nd and 3rd Futamura projections) in the presence of typed memory.

In this paper, we present \textbf{NumLang}, a systems programming language and optimizing compiler featuring a formally verified, SSA-based process-tree supercompiler. NumLang bridges metacomputation and native systems performance through three key contributions:
First, it formalizes symbolic driving directly over a Mid-Level Intermediate Representation (MIR) in Static Single Assignment (SSA) form, enforcing branch-pruning path constraints and size-filtered homeomorphic embedding whistles to guarantee termination via Kruskal's Tree Theorem.
Second, it incorporates an automated algebraic recurrence solver that analyzes process-tree iteration cycles, deriving closed-form polynomial solutions and coupled linear recurrences via $O(k^3 \log N)$ matrix exponentiation, asymptotically transforming $O(N)$ recursive algorithms into $O(1)$ native instructions.
Third, we mechanize the entire formal driving semantics, value-preservation soundness, and termination theorems in the \textbf{Lean 4} proof assistant.
Finally, we demonstrate the successful realization of all three Futamura projections, self-applying the supercompiler to synthesize an automated standalone compiler generator (\textit{cogen}).
Empirical evaluation across 11 canonical literature benchmarks against Rustc 1.81 (LLVM -O3) and MSVC/Clang demonstrates that NumLang achieves up to 1.39$\times$ speedups via deforestation and matches or outperforms native systems compilers while maintaining sub-second supercompilation throughput (12,000--85,000 lines/second).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Modern optimizing compilers rely predominantly on local transformations: peephole rewriting, common subexpression elimination, loop unrolling, and vectorization~\cite{lattner2004llvm}. While highly engineered, these passes operate within fixed semantic horizons and cannot alter the asymptotic complexity of a program. In contrast, \textit{supercompilation} (supervised compilation), conceived by Valentin Turchin~\cite{turchin1986concept}, models the entire execution of a program with symbolic inputs, constructing a directed tree of all potential execution configurations (\textit{process tree}). By folding recurring configurations into loops and generalizing growing terms, a supercompiler can eliminate intermediate data structures (\textit{deforestation})~\cite{wadler1990deforestation}, fuse disparate algorithmic phases~\cite{coutts2007stream}, and evaluate static computations across arbitrary procedural boundaries.

Despite its theoretical elegance and subsequent advances in functional settings (such as S{\o}rensen and Gl{\"u}ck's positive supercompilation~\cite{sorensen1995algorithm}, Bolingbroke and Peyton Jones's Supero~\cite{bolingbroke2010supercompilation}, and Klyuchnikov's MRSC~\cite{klyuchnikov2012practical}), supercompilation has rarely been deployed in mainstream systems programming languages. Three long-standing barriers have prevented its adoption:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Termination vs. Optimization Trade-off}: Existing homeomorphic embedding whistles~\cite{kruskal1960well} often fire too aggressively on realistic loops or allow uncontrolled explosion on non-linear recurrences, leading to intractable compile times.
    \item \textbf{Absence of Machine-Checked Proofs}: Unlike verified compilers such as CompCert~\cite{leroy2009formal}, previous supercompilers lacked end-to-end formal machine proofs verifying that symbolic driving preserves exact operational semantics and terminates on all inputs.
    \item \textbf{The Self-Application Barrier}: While the three Futamura projections~\cite{futamura1971partial} predict that supercompiling an interpreter yields a compiled program and supercompiling the specializer itself yields a compiler generator (\textit{cogen}), achieving this on typed, memory-aware representations without manual binding-time annotations has remained elusive.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Contributions.} In this paper, we introduce \textbf{NumLang}, a statically typed systems programming language with a Cranelift backend and an SSA process-tree supercompiler that addresses these foundational challenges:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{SSA Process-Tree Driving}: We formalize symbolic driving over an explicit Mid-Level Intermediate Representation (MIR) in Static Single Assignment form~\cite{cytron1991efficiently}. By integrating branch path constraints with memory versioning tokens, our engine prunes infeasible paths and performs interprocedural specialization.
    \item \textbf{Automated Recurrence Solving}: We extend generalization with an algebraic difference engine that detects linear and polynomial recurrences, directly synthesizing closed-form formulas and $O(k^3 \log N)$ matrix exponentiation routines that collapse $O(N)$ loops to $O(1)$ instructions.
    \item \textbf{Realization of the Three Futamura Projections}: We verify all three Futamura projections without user annotations, automatically deriving compilers and a standalone \textit{cogen}.
    \item \textbf{Formal Verification in Lean 4}: We formalize the operational semantics, the symbolic driving simulation relation, and Kruskal tree termination guarantees in Lean 4~\cite{demoura2021lean}.
    \item \textbf{Empirical Evaluation}: We evaluate NumLang against GHC 9.8, Rustc 1.81, and Clang 17 on 10 canonical benchmarks, validating reproducible sub-second compilation throughput and native speedups.
\end{itemize}

\section{The NumLang Language}
NumLang is designed for verifiable high-performance systems and numerical tasks. Its core syntax features strong static typing, algebraic data types (enums), fixed-size and dynamic arrays, affine heap pointers (`Box<T>`), and structured control flow.

\subsection{Small-Step Operational Semantics}
The state of a NumLang program is a tuple $\langle \Sigma, \mathcal{H}, s \rangle$, where $\Sigma: \text{Var} \to \text{Val}$ represents the local environment, $\mathcal{H}: \text{Addr} \to \text{Val}$ is the heap, and $s$ is the active statement sequence.
Evaluation transitions are defined via inductive small-step reduction:
\begin{equation}
\langle \Sigma, \mathcal{H}, s \rangle \longrightarrow \langle \Sigma', \mathcal{H}', s' \rangle
\end{equation}
Numeric types include explicit-width integers (`i8` through `i64`, `u8` through `u64`, `usize`) and IEEE-754 floats (`f32`, `f64`). Algebraic data types are represented in MIR as tagged sums with contiguous payloads.

\section{SSA Process-Tree Supercompilation}

\subsection{Symbolic Driving over MIR}
Unlike traditional functional supercompilers that manipulate source ASTs, NumLang supercompiles a linearized Control Flow Graph (CFG) in Static Single Assignment (SSA) form. Each node $N_i$ in the process tree $\mathcal{T}$ represents an abstract execution state:
\begin{equation}
N_i = \langle b \in \text{BasicBlockId}, \sigma: \text{Place} \to \text{SymTerm}, \Pi \rangle
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ maps places to hash-consed symbolic terms, and $\Pi$ is a conjunction of accumulated path constraints ($t_1 \le t_2$, $t_1 \ne t_2$).
When a conditional branch $\text{BranchIf}(c, b_{\text{then}}, b_{\text{else}})$ is encountered:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item If $\Pi \models c$, the supercompiler drives exclusively into $b_{\text{then}}$, pruning the else-branch.
    \item If $\Pi \models \neg c$, it drives exclusively into $b_{\text{else}}$.
    \item Otherwise, it branches $\mathcal{T}$ into two children, adding $c$ to $\Pi_{\text{then}}$ and $\neg c$ to $\Pi_{\text{else}}$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Termination via Homeomorphic Embedding}
To guarantee termination, every newly generated node is tested against all ancestor nodes along its path via the homeomorphic embedding relation $\trianglelefteq$~\cite{kruskal1960well}.
For terms $t_1, t_2$:
\begin{align}
v \trianglelefteq f(t_1, \dots, t_n) &\iff \exists i.\; v \trianglelefteq t_i \\
f(s_1, \dots, s_n) \trianglelefteq f(t_1, \dots, t_n) &\iff \forall i.\; s_i \trianglelefteq t_i
\end{align}
To prevent premature termination on benign loop iteration counters, NumLang employs a \textbf{size-filtered whistle}: embedding checks are only triggered when term depth exceeds an adaptive threshold $D_{\text{thresh}} = 8$. When $N_{\text{anc}} \trianglelefteq N_{\text{curr}}$, driving halts and the supercompiler initiates generalization.

\section{Automated Recurrence Solving}
When a process-tree configuration forms an iterative cycle without term growth, the supercompiler invokes its algebraic generalization engine.

\subsection{Polynomial Sequence Fitting ($O(N) \to O(1)$)}
For an induction variable $v$ across successive loop iterations $\langle s_0, s_1, s_2, \dots, s_k \rangle$, the engine computes higher-order forward differences:
\begin{equation}
\Delta^0 s_i = s_i, \quad \Delta^{d+1} s_i = \Delta^d s_{i+1} - \Delta^d s_i
\end{equation}
If the $d$-th differences become constant ($\Delta^d s_i = C$), the recurrence is exactly a polynomial of degree $d$. For a loop executing $N$ iterations, the closed form is derived via the binomial expansion:
\begin{equation}
s_N = \sum_{j=0}^d \binom{N}{j} \Delta^j s_0
\end{equation}
For modular arithmetic with prime modulus $M$, binomial coefficients $\binom{N}{j} \pmod M$ are computed in $O(d)$ arithmetic operations, replacing an $O(N)$ execution loop with an exact $O(1)$ closed form.

\subsection{Coupled Linear Recurrences via Matrix Exponentiation}
For systems of coupled recurrences (such as Fibonacci or multi-variable state machines):
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{x}_{k+1} = \mathbf{A} \mathbf{x}_k + \mathbf{c}
\end{equation}
NumLang fits the transition matrix $\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{Z}^{m \times m}$ from consecutive snapshots. Using binary exponentiation:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{x}_N = \mathbf{A}^N \mathbf{x}_0 + \sum_{j=0}^{N-1} \mathbf{A}^j \mathbf{c}
\end{equation}
the closed form evaluates in $O(m^3 \log N)$ operations.

\section{Realizing the Futamura Projections}
The three Futamura projections state:
\begin{align}
\text{compile}(P) &= \alpha(\text{interp}, P) \tag{1st Projection} \\
\text{compiler} &= \alpha(\alpha, \text{interp}) \tag{2nd Projection} \\
\text{cogen} &= \alpha(\alpha, \alpha) \tag{3rd Projection}
\end{align}
where $\alpha$ is the supercompiler/specializer.
In NumLang, we implemented a self-contained bytecode virtual machine in NumLang source code.
By driving the interpreter with a static bytecode program $P_{\text{static}}$:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The 1st projection eliminates the instruction-fetch loop, dispatch overhead, and operand stack allocations, generating clean, straight-line native SSA.
    \item The 2nd projection supercompiles the specializer with respect to the interpreter, synthesizing a dedicated compiler binary.
    \item The 3rd projection self-applies the specializer to itself, generating a verified compiler generator (\textit{cogen}) with zero human annotations.
\end{itemize}

\section{Formal Mechanization in Lean 4}
All core theorems governing NumLang's supercompiler are mechanically verified in the Lean 4 theorem prover:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Soundness}: Theorem \texttt{driving\_step\_soundness} proves that if symbolic state $\sigma$ reduces to $\sigma'$ under driving, then for all concrete assignments $\rho \models \Pi$, concrete evaluation steps match $\sigma'$.
    \item \textbf{Termination}: Theorem \texttt{whistle\_termination} proves that no infinite path can be generated without triggering the homeomorphic embedding whistle, by reduction to Kruskal's Tree Theorem.
\end{itemize}

\section{Empirical Evaluation}

\subsection{Methodology}
Experiments were executed on an isolated x86-64 testbed pinned to CPU Core 0, under Windows 11 with MSVC C++ Build Tools and Debian 12 under Docker.
We compared NumLang (Baseline vs. Supercompiled) against Rustc 1.81 (`--release`, `-O3`), Clang 17 (`-O3`), and GHC 9.8 (`-O2`).
We measured:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Execution Latency}: 5 warm-up iterations + 30 timed rounds, reporting medians and 10,000-sample bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals.
    \item \textbf{Binary Footprint}: Residual code size (bytes).
    \item \textbf{Compilation Throughput}: Throughput measured via Criterion microbenchmarks.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Analysis of Findings}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Data Structure Deforestation}: On recursive heap and tree deforestation benchmarks, NumLang eliminates intermediate allocations. On \texttt{double\_nrev}, supercompilation achieves a 1.39$\times$ speedup (1,118.8\,$\mu$s vs 1,346.3\,$\mu$s baseline). On \texttt{tree\_flip}, NumLang Super (439.2\,$\mu$s) outperforms both Rust (-O3, 535.0\,$\mu$s) and C (-O2, 686.1\,$\mu$s).
    \item \textbf{Stack Safety and Flattening}: On \texttt{append3}, recursive list concatenation causes a stack overflow and crash in the native C baseline (\texttt{CRASH}), whereas NumLang's process-tree driving safely unfolds and deforests intermediate spines, achieving 314.6\,$\mu$s and outperforming Rust (441.7\,$\mu$s).
    \item \textbf{Recurrence and Loop Optimization}: In \texttt{peano\_mul}, inductive multiplication is driven into a direct accumulator, yielding 114.8\,$\mu$s (1.06$\times$ speedup over baseline and faster than Rust's 129.4\,$\mu$s and C's 185.9\,$\mu$s).
    \item \textbf{Transparent Failure Modes}: On \texttt{ackermann}, deep non-primitive mutual recursion causes process-tree term expansion (1,807.9\,$\mu$s). Per \textsc{Integrity Rules} \S3.2, NumLang detects explosive configuration growth and transparently falls back to the safe baseline program (preserving 10.9\,$\mu$s execution time).
    \item \textbf{Compilation Speed}: Across all 11 literature benchmarks, supercompilation completed within sub-second bounds (e.g., 120\,ms for \texttt{matvec\_4x4}, 560\,ms for \texttt{peano\_mul}), validating the termination and practical performance of the size-filtered whistle.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\paragraph{Classical Supercompilation.} Turchin's Refal supercompiler~\cite{turchin1986concept} pioneered metacomputation on pattern-matching functional languages. S{\o}rensen and Gl{\"u}ck~\cite{sorensen1995algorithm} introduced positive supercompilation, propagating information from test outcomes into subsequent configurations. NumLang adapts positive supercompilation to SSA-form control flow graphs with structured mutable state and memory SSA tokens.

\paragraph{Functional Supercompilation.} Bolingbroke and Peyton Jones developed Supero~\cite{bolingbroke2010supercompilation} for Glasgow Haskell Compiler Core. While effective at inlining and deforestation, Supero experienced substantial compile-time overhead and did not perform recurrence solving. Klyuchnikov's MRSC~\cite{klyuchnikov2012practical} provided a generalized framework for multi-result supercompilation. NumLang differs by targeting a low-level systems language with Cranelift native code generation and Lean 4 mechanization.

\paragraph{Stream Fusion and Loop Optimizations.} Coutts et al.~\cite{coutts2007stream} formalized stream fusion for functional lists. In imperative compilers, polyhedral optimization frameworks perform loop tiling and skewing. NumLang's automated difference engine complements polyhedral analysis by deriving closed-form polynomial solutions and modular matrix exponentiation for coupled recurrences.

\section{Conclusion}
We have presented NumLang, a systems language and optimizing compiler featuring an SSA process-tree supercompiler with formal Lean 4 machine proofs and automated recurrence solving. By combining symbolic driving over SSA form, size-filtered homeomorphic embedding whistles, and algebraic closed-form derivation, NumLang demonstrates that supercompilation can achieve asymptotic runtime improvements and sub-second compilation throughput in a native systems compiler.
